Part 1 — Foundations of Everyday Conversation

Most conversations are won or lost in the first and last thirty seconds. Learn to open, sustain, and close naturally — then practice it below.

1.1 Greetings — from casual to formal

RegisterWhat you sayWhen
Very casualHey! / What's up? / How's it going?Friends, close colleagues
CasualHi! How are you doing? / How have you been?Your safe everyday default
WarmHey, good to see you! / Great to see you again.Someone you know and like
Neutral / workGood morning. How are you today?Colleagues, guests, stand-up
FormalGood morning. It's a pleasure to see you.Senior leaders, VIP guests
The trap Vietnamese speakers fall into
"How are you?" is usually a greeting, not a real question. Keep the answer short and light: "Good, thanks — you?" Launching into a full report ("I am fine. And you? I am fine too.") sounds robotic. Keep it breezy and bounce it back.

1.2 Goodbyes

  • Casual: See you! / Catch you later / Take care
  • Warm: "It was great catching up — let's do this again soon."
  • Work: "Have a good one." / "Have a great rest of your day."
  • Leaving a meeting: "Thanks, everyone. I'll follow up by email."
  • Formal: "It was a pleasure meeting you. I look forward to staying in touch."

"Have a good one" is a hugely common, friendly sign-off — add it to your rotation.

1.3 Small talk starters

  • Weather: "Lovely morning, isn't it?" / "Can you believe this heat?"
  • The weekend: "Did you get up to anything over the weekend?" / "Any plans for the weekend?"
  • Work: "How's your week going?" / "Busy day?"
  • Shared situation: "Have you been waiting long?" / "First time here?"
  • Compliment: "I love your bag — where's it from?"
The magic follow-up
Don't just answer — throw the ball back. "Any plans for the weekend?" → "Yeah, hiking with some friends. You?" That little "You?" keeps the rally going.

1.4 Introductions

You: Hi, I don't think we've met — I'm Kris.
Them: Nice to meet you, Kris. I'm Daniel.
You: Good to meet you, Daniel. So what do you do here?
  • "Let me introduce myself — I'm Kris, I work in Learning and Development."
  • "Have you two met? Daniel, this is Kris."
  • "I've heard so much about you." (warm and flattering)
  • Responses: "Nice to meet you." / "Pleasure." / "Likewise." (= me too — sounds polished)

1.5 Keeping the conversation alive

a) Follow-up questions

"Oh really? How come?" / "That sounds interesting — tell me more." / "What made you get into that?"

b) Active-listening sounds

"Mm-hmm." "Right." "Oh wow." "No way!" "That makes sense." "Exactly." — sprinkle these in and you instantly sound more engaged.

c) Echo + expand

Them: I just got back from Da Lat.
You: Da Lat? Nice — how was it?

1.6 Ending a conversation gracefully

  • "Anyway, I should let you go —" (classic, very polite)
  • "I'd better get back to it, but it was great chatting."
  • "Listen, I've got to run, but let's catch up soon."
  • "It was really nice talking to you — take care!"

Part 2 — The Functional Toolkit

The "moves" of conversation: opinions, agreeing, disagreeing, requests, apologies — each with a ladder from casual to polite.

2.1 Giving your opinion (stop saying only "I think")

  • "If you ask me, …" / "The way I see it, …"
  • "Honestly, I feel like …" / "I'd say …"
  • "In my experience, …" (great at work — sounds credible)
  • Stronger: "I'm convinced that …" · Softer: "I might be wrong, but …"

2.2 Agreeing

StrengthPhrases
TotalAbsolutely. / Exactly. / Couldn't agree more. / You're spot on.
NormalYeah, definitely. / That's true. / Good point. / Fair enough.
PartialI see your point, but … / True, though … / Yes and no.
Sound native today
"Fair enough." = "Okay, that's reasonable." Incredibly common and flexible. "You want to leave early? Fair enough."

2.3 Disagreeing — politely

The formula: acknowledge + soften + your view.

  • "I see what you mean, but I'm not sure I agree."
  • "That's a fair point, though I'd look at it differently."
  • "Hmm, I'm not so sure about that."
  • Very gentle: "I'm probably missing something, but wouldn't it be better to …?"

2.4 Asking for clarification

  • "Sorry, could you say that again?" / "Sorry, I didn't catch that."
  • "When you say X, do you mean …?"
  • "Sorry, I'm not quite following. Could you walk me through it again?"
  • One word missed: "Sorry, the… what?" (rising tone)

2.5 Checking they understand YOU

  • "Does that make sense?" (soft, common)
  • "Let me know if I'm going too fast."
  • "Feel free to stop me if anything's unclear."

2.6 Requests — the politeness ladder

PolitenessRequest
Direct (peers)Can you pass me that? / Give me a hand with this?
PoliteCould you help me with this? / Would you mind sending it over?
Very politeI was wondering if you could possibly … / Would it be possible to …?
Softening add-ons… when you get a chance / if it's not too much trouble / no rush.
"Would you mind…" trap
The polite "yes" is actually "No, not at all!" (= I don't mind). When in doubt, answer with an action word: "Sure, of course!" / "Not at all, go ahead."

2.7 Offers & invitations

  • Offering: "Can I get you anything?" / "Do you want a hand?" / "Let me get that for you."
  • Inviting: "Do you fancy grabbing a coffee?" (BrE) / "Want to grab lunch?" (AmE)
  • Accepting: "That'd be great, thanks!" / "I'd love to." / "Sounds good."
  • Declining kindly: "I'd love to, but I can't make it this time." / "Thanks, but I'm good."

2.8 Making suggestions

  • "Why don't we …?" / "How about we …?" / "What if we …?"
  • "We could always …" (relaxed, low-pressure)
  • "It might be worth checking with HR first."

2.9 Apologizing

SituationWhat to say
TinySorry! / Oops, my bad.
NormalI'm so sorry about that. / Sorry to keep you waiting.
Work / sincereI apologize for the confusion. / That's on me — I'll fix it.
SeriousI want to sincerely apologize for … It won't happen again.

"That's on me" is a confident, mature way to take responsibility at work.

2.10 Thanking & responding

  • Thanks: "Thanks so much." / "I really appreciate it." / "You're a lifesaver."
  • Replies: "No problem." / "Anytime." / "My pleasure." / "Sure thing."

2.11 Interrupting & holding the floor

  • Interrupting: "Sorry to jump in, but …" / "Can I just add something?"
  • Holding your turn: "Just to finish my thought —"
  • Giving the floor: "Sorry, you were saying?" / "Go ahead."

2.12 Complaining & handling complaints

  • Complaining (firm but kind): "I'm afraid there's been a bit of a problem with …" / "Is there anything you can do about it?"
  • Handling: "I'm so sorry to hear that — let me see what I can do." / "Thank you for bringing this to my attention."

Part 3 — Sounding Natural

The part that separates "correct English" from "native-sounding English": fillers, connectors, softeners, and blended speech.

3.1 Fillers & thinking time

Never freeze in silence or say "how to say…". Buy time out loud:

  • "Well…" / "Let me think…" / "Let's see…"
  • "You know…" / "I mean…" / "Like…" (don't overuse "like")
  • "How can I put this…" / "What's the word…"

3.2 Discourse markers — the connectors of speech

FunctionMarkersExample
AddingPlus, / On top of that,Plus, it's cheaper.
ContrastingThat said, / Then again,That said, it's not perfect.
Giving reasonThe thing is,The thing is, we're short on time.
Changing topicAnyway, / By the way, / Speaking of which,Anyway, how's the family?
ConcludingSo basically, / At the end of the day,At the end of the day, it worked out.
Softening bad newsTo be honest, / I'm afraid…To be honest, it's not looking great.
Two phrases that make you sound fluent instantly
"At the end of the day…" = ultimately. "At the end of the day, guests just want to feel cared for."
"The thing is…" = introduces the real point. "I'd love to help — the thing is, I'm fully booked today."

3.3 Softeners & hedging

  • Downtoners: kind of, sort of, a bit, slightly. "It's a bit expensive."
  • Softening opinions: "I'd say…", "It seems…", "probably", "I guess."
  • Softening requests: "Could you just double-check this?"

Compare: "You're wrong." vs. "I think you might be slightly off there." Same message — completely different social effect.

3.4 Connected speech (why natives sound "fast")

WrittenSounds likeExample
going togonnaI'm gonna call her.
want towannaDo you wanna come?
got togottaI gotta go.
let melemmeLemme check.
give megimmeGimme a second.
what are youwhatchaWhatcha doing?
did youdidjaDidja see that?
kind ofkindaIt's kinda cold.
Speak vs. write
Use these out loud, never in emails or reports. Pronouncing "going to" as two crisp words in casual speech is a classic non-native tell.

3.5 Reactions & backchanneling

  • Surprise: "Oh really?" / "No way!" / "Seriously?" / "You're kidding!"
  • Good news: "That's amazing!" / "Good for you!" / "Nice!"
  • Bad news: "Oh no." / "I'm so sorry to hear that." / "That must've been tough."
  • Agreement: "Right." / "Exactly." / "Totally." / "That makes sense."
  • Neutral listening: "Mm-hmm." / "Gotcha." / "I see."

3.6 Vague language (yes, it sounds MORE natural)

  • "…and stuff." / "…and things like that." / "…or whatever."
  • "a couple of" (= 2 or a few) · "a bunch of" (= many)
  • "or so" — "I'll be there in ten minutes or so."
  • "-ish" — "Let's meet at 7-ish." / "fifty-ish people" / "I'm hungry-ish."
"-ish" is your friend
Adding "-ish" to times, numbers and adjectives is extremely common and instantly casual/native. Use it and you'll sound like a local.

Part 5 — Professional & Workplace English

Meetings, emails, facilitating training, feedback — tuned to your daily L&D reality.

5.1 Meetings

Opening

  • "Thanks for joining, everyone. Let's get started."
  • "The main thing I'd like to cover today is …"

Contributing

  • "Can I add something here?" / "Building on what Phuong said, …"
  • "From an L&D perspective, …" (frames your expertise)
  • "Just to play devil's advocate for a second …"

Managing the discussion

  • "Let's park that for now and come back to it."
  • "Can we take that offline?" (= discuss separately)
  • "To bring us back on track, …"

Closing

  • "So, to sum up, the action items are …"
  • "Who's owning this?" / "I'll send round a quick recap."
Phrases that make you sound senior
"Let's take that offline." · "Let's park it." · "Who owns this?" · "Let's circle back on Friday." — these signal you run tight, action-oriented meetings.

5.2 Email & chat language

PurposeNatural phrasing
Opening (warm)Hi Jamie, / Hope you're doing well.
Opening (purpose)Just a quick note about … / Following up on …
RequestingCould you please …? / When you get a chance, could you …
AttachingI've attached … / Here's the … you asked for.
Chasing politelyJust checking in on this. / Any update on …?
ClosingThanks so much, / Best, / Kind regards,
Softening in email
"Just" and "quick" are your softeners: "just a quick question." But don't over-apologize — one light softener is plenty. Too much "sorry" makes you sound unsure.

5.3 Presenting & facilitating training

Opening a session

  • "Welcome, everyone — great to see you all here."
  • "By the end of today, you'll be able to …" (state the outcome)
  • "Feel free to jump in with questions as we go."

Guiding

  • "Let's dive into the first topic." / "Moving on to …"
  • "This next part is really important, so bear with me."

Involving the room

  • "What are your thoughts on this?" / "Has anyone experienced this?"
  • "Great point — can you say a bit more about that?"

Checking & closing

  • "Is everyone with me so far?"
  • "Let's recap the three key takeaways."
  • "Thanks so much for your energy today — that's a wrap."

5.4 Giving & receiving feedback

  • Positive: "You did a really nice job on …, especially …"
  • Constructive: "One thing I'd suggest is …" / "It might be even stronger if …"
  • "Have you considered …?" (invites, doesn't command)
  • Receiving: "That's really helpful, thank you." / "Good point — I hadn't thought of it that way."

5.5 Networking

  • "So, what do you do?" → answer with a hook: "I'm in L&D at a resort — basically I help new hires learn the ropes."
  • "How did you get into that?" (a great question to ask)
  • "We should stay in touch — are you on LinkedIn?"
"Learn the ropes"
= learn how things are done in a new job. "Show someone the ropes" = train them. Perfect vocabulary for your role.

Part 6 — Hospitality English

Built for your world at The Westin — warm, gracious, guest-first. Also perfect models for training your associates.

6.1 Welcoming & guest interactions

  • "Welcome to The Westin — it's wonderful to have you with us."
  • "My pleasure." (the hospitality gold standard — warmer than "no problem")
  • "Is there anything else I can do for you?"
  • "Please don't hesitate to let me know if you need anything at all."
  • "Enjoy the rest of your stay." / "Have a lovely evening."
Upgrade your service words
"Certainly" instead of "OK" · "My pleasure" instead of "No problem" · "Right away" instead of "Now" · "I'd be happy to" instead of "I can" · "Absolutely" instead of "Yes." Small words, big class.

6.2 Front desk / check-in

Associate: Good afternoon, welcome to The Westin. Do you have a reservation with us?
Guest: Yes, under the name Tan.
Associate: Wonderful, Mr. Tan. I have you down for three nights in a Deluxe Ocean View. May I see your ID, please?
Guest: Here you go.
Associate: Thank you. You're all set. Your room is on the eighth floor — here are your key cards. Breakfast is served until 10:30. Is there anything else I can help you with?

Concierge / directions

  • "Of course — let me point you in the right direction."
  • "It's just a short walk / a five-minute drive from here."
  • "I'd highly recommend … It's very popular with our guests."

6.3 Handling complaints — the recovery sequence

Listen → Empathize → Apologize → Act → Follow up

StepWhat to say
Listen"I'm sorry to hear that. Please, tell me exactly what happened."
Empathize"I completely understand — that's not the experience we want for you."
Apologize"I sincerely apologize for the inconvenience."
Act"Here's what I'm going to do right away … / Let me sort this out for you personally."
Follow up"I'll check back shortly to make sure everything's been resolved."
The phrase that de-escalates
"Let me see what I can do for you." It doesn't over-promise, but it signals ownership and action — exactly what an upset guest needs to hear.

6.4 Loyalty & Elite (Bonvoy) language

  • "Thank you for being a valued Bonvoy member — we truly appreciate your loyalty."
  • "As a Platinum Elite member, you're entitled to …"
  • "We've upgraded you as a thank-you for your continued loyalty."
  • "It's our pleasure to welcome you back."

Elite guests want to feel seen — words like "valued," "appreciate," "welcome back," and "loyalty" do the emotional work.

6.5 Phone & reservation English

You: Good morning, thank you for calling The Westin Resort. This is Kris speaking — how may I help you?
Caller: Hi, I'd like to check availability for next weekend.
You: Certainly, I'd be happy to help. May I have the dates and the number of guests, please?
Caller: The 12th to the 14th, two adults.
You: Let me just check that for you… Yes, we do have availability. Would you like me to go ahead and book that?
  • "May I ask who's calling?" / "Could you hold for just a moment?"
  • "I'll put you through to …" / "Let me take a message."
  • "Could you spell that for me, please?"

Part 7 — Real-Life Dialogues to Shadow

Don't read silently — shadow: say each line out loud, copy the rhythm, then reproduce the scene from memory. Then hit Roleplay Mode below: your lines are hidden — speak first, reveal after!

7.1 Catching up with a colleague over coffee

Kris: Hey! Long time no see — how have you been?
Anh: Pretty good, thanks! Busy as always. You?
Kris: Can't complain. Work's been full-on with Culture Week coming up.
Anh: Oh nice — are you running it again this year?
Kris: Yeah, MC-ing the whole thing. A bit nerve-racking, honestly, but I kind of love it.
Anh: You'll smash it. You're a natural.
Kris: Ha, we'll see! Anyway, enough about me — how's the new role treating you?
Phrases to steal
"Long time no see" · "Can't complain" (= I'm doing well) · "full-on" (= very busy) · "You'll smash it" (= you'll do great) · "how's X treating you?"

7.2 A job interview (competency question)

Interviewer: Can you tell me about a time you handled a difficult situation at work?
Kris: Sure. So, in my current role, we once had a training program fall behind schedule because several trainers dropped out at short notice.
Interviewer: And how did you handle that?
Kris: Well, the first thing I did was reassess what was actually essential. I reached out to two colleagues to fill in, restructured the sessions, and kept my manager in the loop throughout. In the end, we delivered on time, and the feedback was actually stronger than the year before.
Interviewer: What did you take away from it?
Kris: Honestly, that staying calm and communicating early matters more than having a perfect plan. Things go wrong — what counts is how quickly you adapt.

Notice the STAR shape: Situation → Task → Action → Result. And the natural fillers ("So," "Well," "Honestly") that make it sound spoken, not memorized.

7.3 Disagreeing in a meeting — without friction

Colleague: I think we should cut the orientation down to half a day to save time.
Kris: I see where you're coming from, and I agree we're tight on time. That said, I'd be a bit cautious about cutting orientation — it's where new hires form their first impression of the brand.
Colleague: Fair, but people do complain it's long.
Kris: True. What if we kept the full day but made the afternoon more interactive? That way we keep the depth without losing people's attention.
Colleague: Actually, that's not a bad idea.
The recipe in action
Acknowledge ("I see where you're coming from") → soften ("I'd be a bit cautious") → your view with a reason → a middle path ("What if we…"). Wins people over instead of putting them on the defensive.

7.4 Handling an upset guest

Guest: This is ridiculous. I've been waiting forty minutes for room service.
Kris: I'm so sorry, sir — forty minutes is far too long, and I completely understand why you're frustrated.
Guest: I just want my food.
Kris: Absolutely. Let me chase that down for you right now and make sure it's on its way. As an apology for the wait, I'd like to take the delivery charge off your bill.
Guest: Well… okay, thank you.
Kris: Of course. I'll personally follow up in a few minutes to make sure everything's arrived. Again, my sincere apologies.

7.5 Networking at a conference

Kris: Hi, mind if I join you? These sessions are great but I don't know a soul here.
Mai: Ha, same! Please, go ahead. I'm Mai.
Kris: Nice to meet you, Mai — I'm Kris. So what brings you to the conference?
Mai: I'm in HR at a hotel group. You?
Kris: Oh, small world — I'm in Learning and Development, also hospitality. What's your take on the keynote?
Mai: Loved it, actually. The bit about coaching stuck with me.
Kris: Same here. Hey, we should stay in touch — are you on LinkedIn?
Networking openers that always work
"Mind if I join you?" · "I don't know a soul here." · "What brings you here?" · "Small world!" · "What's your take on…?" · "We should stay in touch."

Part 8 — Pronunciation & Intonation Essentials

You don't need a "perfect" accent — clarity and natural rhythm matter far more. Chosen for Vietnamese speakers specifically.

8.1 Word stress

  • PHO-to-graph → pho-TO-gra-pher → pho-to-GRAPH-ic (stress moves!)
  • Nouns vs. verbs: PRE-sent (gift) vs. pre-SENT (verb); RE-cord vs. re-CORD.
  • Words ending -tion / -sion: stress just before it — informA-tion, decIsion.

8.2 Sentence stress & rhythm

English is stress-timed: stress the content words, rush over the small ones.

"I NEED to TALK to the MANager about the BOOKing." — the little words shrink: "to" → "tuh", "and" → "'n".

The #1 rhythm fix for Vietnamese speakers
Vietnamese gives every syllable equal weight and a tone. English does not. If you say every syllable with full stress, you'll sound "choppy." Practice deliberately squashing the small words and stretching the important ones — this single change makes a huge difference.

8.3 Intonation (the melody)

  • Falling ↘ — statements & wh-questions: "Where do you live?"
  • Rising ↗ — yes/no questions & uncertainty: "Are you coming?"
  • Fall-rise ↗↘ — politeness / "but…": "It's fine… (but not great)."
  • Listing ↗↗↘ — rise on each item, fall on the last: "pens, paper, and a projector."

8.4 Sounds that trip up Vietnamese speakers

SoundThe issuePractice
Final consonantsOften dropped/softenedLand on the END: "cat," "book," "eight."
-s / -es / -ed endingsFrequently droppedbooks, wants, needed — don't swallow the ending.
/θ/ and /ð/ (th)No Vietnamese equivalentTongue between teeth: think, this, three, mother.
/l/ vs /n/ at word endOften mergedContrast: "fine/file," "seen/seal."
Consonant clustersHard togetherstreet, texts, asked — slow, then speed up.
/v/ vs /w/, final /z/Merged or droppedvery/wary; "is, was, does" end in /z/.

8.5 Linking (why speech flows)

  • Consonant + vowel: "an apple" → "a-napple"; "pick it up" → "pi-ki-tup".
  • Same consonants merge: "big game" → one long g.
  • Vowel + vowel gets a tiny glide: "go on" → "go-won"; "I am" → "I-yam".

Part 9 — Common Mistakes Vietnamese Learners Make

Nothing shameful here — they come from real differences between the languages. Fix even half of these and you'll noticeably level up.

9.1 Grammar patterns

Instead of…Say…Why
I very like it.I really like it."Very" doesn't modify verbs.
I have 25 years old.I am 25.Age uses "be," not "have."
I very tired yesterday.I was very tired yesterday.Don't drop "be."
Yesterday I go to work.Yesterday I went to work.Past time → past tense.
She go to school.She goes to school.3rd-person adds -s.
I no have time.I don't have time.Negatives need do/does/did.
I will can do it.I'll be able to do it.Two modals can't stack.
If I will have time, I call.If I have time, I'll call.No "will" in the if-clause.
I'm boring in this class.I'm bored in this class.-ed = your feeling; -ing = the cause.
I'm agree with you.I agree with you."Agree" is a verb; no "am."

9.2 Articles & prepositions

  • "a/an" for one non-specific thing the first time: "I saw a dog."
  • "the" when both people know which one: "The dog was huge."
  • No article for general plurals/uncountables: "I like coffee." "Dogs are loyal."

The mindset shift
The biggest leap isn't memorizing rules — it's stopping word-by-word translation. Learn whole English chunks ("I don't think so," "I'll do my best") and reach for the chunk directly.

Part 10 — Your 30-Day Fluency Plan

Knowledge you don't practice fades. Most days need only 15–20 focused minutes. And now your daily workout lives right here.

The daily core (every day, ~15 min)

  1. Warm-up (2 min): read yesterday's phrases out loud once.
  2. Learn (5 min): pick 3–5 new phrases from one section. Say each aloud 3 times.
  3. Shadow (5 min): run one Roleplay dialogue from Part 7 out loud.
  4. Use it (3 min): do the Daily Quick 5 below, then use one phrase in a real conversation.

The 4-week map

WeekFocusGoal by end of week
Week 1Parts 1–2: greetings, small talk, functional toolkitOpen, sustain, and close a chat smoothly.
Week 2Part 3: sounding naturalFillers and softeners come automatically; "gonna/wanna" out loud.
Week 3Parts 4–6: vocabulary, work & hospitality EnglishUse 20 new phrasal verbs; run a mock meeting or guest scenario.
Week 4Parts 7–9: shadow dialogues, fix your mistakesRoleplay all dialogues from memory; kill 5 recurring errors.

Habits that separate fluent people from stuck people

  • Speak before you're ready. Waiting until your English is "good enough" means waiting forever.
  • Being understood beats being perfect. Communication is the goal; grammar is the servant.
  • Learn phrases, not just words. Fluency is stored in chunks.
  • Get comfortable being wrong out loud. Every spoken mistake is a mistake corrected faster.
  • Consistency beats intensity. 15 minutes daily beats 3 hours weekly, every time.
